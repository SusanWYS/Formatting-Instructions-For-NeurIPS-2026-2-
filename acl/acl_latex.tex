\begin{abstract}
Humans often infer causal relationships that are suggested but never directly stated. For example, “Melissa detests the children who are arrogant and rude” suggests that Melissa detests the children \emph{because} they are arrogant and rude. We ask whether language models make similar implicit causal inferences and whether these inferences are represented internally. Across seven language models, we find that implicit causal information reliably changes what models expect to come next, even when models do not always report the same inference when asked directly. We also find an internal representation of causality that can be distinguished from plausibility and occasion compatibility. This representation transfers from implicit causal relations across sentences to causal relations within a sentence. Moreover, demographic descriptions shift this causal signal in positive and negative social scenarios, although the direction and magnitude vary across models. Together, these results suggest that implicit causal inferences are reflected both in model behavior and in internal representations that generalize across linguistic contexts and reveal systematic differences in socially implicated settings.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Language often leaves relationships between events unstated. For example, ``The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired'' suggests that the falsification \emph{caused} the firing, without directly stating so. These optional sentence-internal inferences are known as \emph{elicitures} \citep{cohen-kehler-2021-conversational}. While a substantial body of work has investigated whether language models (LMs) capture pragmatic phenomena such as conversational implicatures \citep{ruis2023goldilocks, jeretic-etal-2020-natural, schuster-etal-2020-harnessing, li-etal-2021-predicting}, elicitures have received less attention \citep[but see][]{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}, and are potentially derived through different mechanisms. Some theories argue that, in contrast to implicature, elicitures arise due to coherence and world knowledge rather than violation of conversational norms \citep{cohen-kehler-2021-conversational,rohde-etal-2011-anticipating}. Therefore, understanding how LMs represent implicit causal relationships can shed light on their internal world models, including in scenarios where causal inferences have real-world impacts.

We study the implicit causal inferences in LMs through four main research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} \textit{Do LM behaviors reflect inferences about implicit causal relationships within sentences?} \textbf{RQ2:} \textit{Do LMs form internal representations of implicit causal relationships that are distinct from other relations?} And if so, \textbf{RQ3:} \textit{Does the identified internal representation causally influence downstream sentence processing?} Connecting to the broader theme of alignment, \textbf{RQ4:} \textit{Do these representations generalize from cross-sentence causal relations to within-sentence causal inferences?}

In a related study, \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic} show that LMs infer implicit causality via eliciture and use it during subsequent sentence processing (akin to our RQ1). RQ1 extends Pan \& Kehler's behavioral study by testing different syntactic constructions. Furthermore, we study whether implicit causality forms an internal representation that is distinct from occasion compatibility and plausibility, and whether this representation generalizes across syntactic constructions (RQ2). We validate that the identified internal directions are causally used by models in RQ3. We separately examine whether the same representation varies with demographic information in naturalistic social scenarios (RQ4).

Across seven language models tested, we find that implicit causality consistently shapes model behavior and is reflected in internal representations that generalize across linguistic constructions. These representations causally influence downstream sentence processing and vary systematically with demographic descriptions. More broadly, this study reveals how LMs represent causal relationships that are inferred rather than stated,
% Our results can shed light on how models construct internal world models, how their nonliteral inferences align with human cognition, and whether these representations encode systematic biases with
with potential implications for the development of safer and more aligned models.

\subsection{Measuring implicit causal inference through model behaviors}
\label{sec:behavior}
We first ask whether implicit causal inferences influence model behavior. To test this, we compare two kinds of direct metalinguistic judgments with two measures based on the surprisal of subsequent texts \citep{hu-levy-2023-prompting}. Our setup replicates and extends \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}. More details about behavioral evaluation are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:behavior-details}.

\paragraph{Data.}

To test LM implicit causality attribution, we manually authored 150 minimal pairs with the structure \textbf{[person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome]}. In the critical member, the modifier provides a plausible cause of the outcome (e.g., \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired}); in the control, it provides an incidental detail (e.g., \emph{The executive who wore a blue silk tie was fired}).

\paragraph{Methods.} We use two direct prompted judgments and two surprisal-based measures. 

Under the \textbf{Yes/No (direct)} measure, we present the sentence and ask, ``Does [modifier/relative clause] explain why [outcome]?'' The model is instructed to answer Yes or No. Let $c$ be the resulting prompt, and define the complete answer string as $a$. We sum the probability $P_\theta(a\mid c)$ over different capitalization variants of Yes and No and renormalize over all candidates:
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{YN}}(c)=
\frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}}P_\theta(a\mid c)}
{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{N}}P_\theta(a\mid c)},
\label{eq:yesno-main}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Y}=\{\texttt{ yes},\texttt{ Yes},\texttt{ YES}\}$ and $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{ no},\texttt{ No},\texttt{ NO}\}$. Thus, $s_{\mathrm{YN}}$ measures the model's relative support for Yes among these six strings.

For the \textbf{7-point Likert (direct)} measure, we ask how strongly the modifier explains the outcome, from 1 (definitely not) through 4 (uncertain) to 7 (definitely yes). Let $a_k$ be the complete answer string $ k$ ($k$ is an integer from 1 to 7). We renormalize the probabilities of the seven candidates:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pi_k(c)&=\frac{P_\theta(a_k\mid c)}
{\sum_{j=1}^{7}P_\theta(a_j\mid c)},\\
s_{\mathrm{L}}(c)&=\sum_{k=1}^{7}k\,\pi_k(c).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:likert-main}
\end{equation}

The two remaining measures do not directly prompt the models to attribute causality. For \textbf{IDK Why (surprisal)}, adapted from \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}, we retain the sentence-final period, append the exact string \emph{ I don't know why.}, and sum surprisal over every token in that continuation. If the modifier already explains the outcome, the continuation should be less expected and having lower log probability.

For \textbf{Because-And (surprisal)}, we rewrite each item as the outcome followed by the modifier content, joined with either \emph{because} or \emph{and}. For example, the sentence  \emph{The executive who wore a blue silk tie was fired} yields \emph{The executive was fired because the executive wore a blue silk tie.} and \emph{The executive was fired, and the executive wore a blue silk tie.} We score the same modifier tokens, including the final period, after each connective. The score is their mean log probability after \emph{because} minus their mean log probability after \emph{and}. Larger values indicate a stronger preference for an directly causal connection. We expect the critical member of a pair to have the larger score (larger log probability gap).
\paragraph{Metrics.}
For all four measures, paired accuracy is the proportion of pairs in which the critical item receives a strictly higher score than its control with ties counting as incorrect. See Appendix~\ref{app:behavior-details} for prompts and token-level definitions.

\paragraph{Models.} In all experiments, we test the base checkpoints of Gemma~2 2B/9B \citep{gemma-team-2024-gemma2}, Qwen3 4B/8B \citep{qwen-team-2025-qwen3}, Llama~3.2 3B \citep{meta-2024-llama32}, and Llama~3 8B and Llama~3.1 70B \citep{dubey-etal-2024-llama3}.

\paragraph{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} shows that both surprisal measures achieve consistently high paired accuracy across model families. Accuracy ranges from 90.0\% to 99.3\% for IDK Why and from 82.7\% to 92.0\% for Because-And. In contrast, direct Yes/No and Likert judgments vary substantially, ranging from below chance to near ceiling. Thus, causal information reliably affects models' next-word predictions even when the models do not report the same inference consistently through direct judgments.

\subsection{Identifying internal representations of implicit causality inference}
\label{sec:representations}

The behavioral results show that implicit causal relations affect model predictions, but they do not reveal how the inference is represented in model internals. How do we test if implicit causality is selectively represented in LM activation states? To this end, we find model directions related to implicit causal inferences through contrastive training sets. We further evaluate if a representation learned from cross-sentence causal relationships generalize to the weaker, non-mandated causal readings of within-sentence inferences.

\paragraph{Data.} To test if implicit causality is selectively represented in model internals, we use three datasets: Causal, Occasion (baseline), and Plausibility (baseline), all being matched critical/control pairs.

First, the main \textbf{Causal} dataset (480 pairs) tests implicit causality \emph{across} sentences. For training and in-distribution tuning/testing, we use \emph{cross-sentence} causality because many theories propose that clauses in discourse (here, sentences) must be structurally related \citep[e.g.,][]{hobbs_literature_1990,kehler_coherence_2002}, in contrast to the non-mandated causal relationships between elements \emph{within} a sentence. This approach allows us to test whether causality representations learned from discourse relationships generalize to within-sentence causality (i.e., eliciture). In each pair of cross-sentence constructions, an \emph{explanation or incidental detail sentence} is followed by an \emph{outcome sentence}. For example, the critical item \emph{The potatoes sat in flooded fields for weeks. The potatoes rotted underground.} is paired with the control \emph{The potatoes grew near a wooden property line. The potatoes rotted underground.}
% We constructed 480 pairs: 384 training, 48 development, and 48 test. %held-out in-distribution test.
For out-of-distribution evaluation, we use the 150 within-sentence pairs from Section~\ref{sec:behavior}, where a potential explanation or incidental detail appears as the modifier or relative clause for the outcome.

Second, the \textbf{Occasion (baseline)} dataset (1200 pairs) tests whether an event is compatible with a person's typical activity. We adapt and expand the occasion items from \citet{hu-rubiofernandez-2026-referential} and standardize every evaluated sentence to begin with a capital letter and end with a period. For instance, \emph{A lifeguard got a painful sunburn last Sunday} is occasion-compatible, whereas \emph{A translator got a painful sunburn last Sunday} is its control.

Finally, the \textbf{Plausibility (baseline)} dataset (353 pairs) tests whether an event is physically and semantically possible. We combine 70 probable--impossible pairs from \citet{hu-etal-2025-shades}, 128 pairs from EventsAdapt \citep{kauf-etal-2023-event}, and 155 pairs from \citet{vega-mendoza-etal-2021-concurrent}. Together, these sources provide a broad event-plausiblity baseline. An example contrastive pair is \emph{Someone baked a cake inside an oven} vs \emph{Someone baked a cake inside a freezer}. See Appendix~\ref{app:representation-examples} for further examples.

We use Occasion and Plausibility as baselines because both require world knowledge but do not encode causal relations. Specifically, Occasion evokes another type of non-causal eliciture as described in \citet{hu-rubiofernandez-2026-referential}. They therefore help test whether the causal direction captures causality specifically, rather than general semantic compatibility or event likelihood.
\paragraph{CAA directions.} We use contrastive activation addition (CAA) \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering} to identify an activation-space direction for each relation. Let $\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{crit}}_{i,\ell}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ctrl}}_{i,\ell}$ be the activations at the final non-padding token of the critical and control members of training pair $i$ at layer $\ell$. For $N_{\mathrm{train}}$ pairs, we define the CAA direction as the mean paired difference
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{train}}}
\left(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{crit}}_{i,\ell}-\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ctrl}}_{i,\ell}\right).
\label{eq:caa-direction-main}
\end{equation}
For Plausibility, we compute a direction separately for each of the three source datasets and average the unit-normalized source directions with equal weight:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{plaus}}_{\ell}
=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{r=1}^{3}
\frac{\mathbf{v}^{(r)}_{\ell}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}^{(r)}_{\ell}\rVert_2}.
\label{eq:plausibility-direction-main}
\end{equation}
We score an unseen item $x$ by projecting its final-token activation onto the unit-normalized direction,
\begin{equation}
s_{\ell}(x)=\mathbf{h}_{\ell}(x)^{\top}\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\ell}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\rVert_2}.
\label{eq:caa-score-main}
\end{equation}
For an evaluation set of $N_{\mathrm{eval}}$ matched pairs, pairwise accuracy is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{i,\ell}&=s_{\ell}(x_i^{\mathrm{crit}})-s_{\ell}(x_i^{\mathrm{ctrl}}),\\
a_{i,\ell}&=\mathbf{1}(d_{i,\ell}>0)
  +\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}(d_{i,\ell}=0),\\
\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{pair}}(\ell)
  &=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}a_{i,\ell}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:caa-accuracy-main}
\end{equation}
Thus, an evaluation pair is correct when its critical item projects farther along the direction than its control; an exact tie receives half credit. We select the layer with the highest development-set paired accuracy and report accuracy at that fixed layer on the disjoint test set. For Plausibility, layer selection and test accuracy use an unweighted macro-average over the three sources. See Appendix~\ref{app:caa-details} for the candidate layers and further implementation details.

\paragraph{Results.}
The CAA directions are relatively relation-selective: each direction strongly separates its own held-out relation, whereas cross-relation transfer is substantially weaker and more variable (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-transfer}). This selectivity is not absolute, indicating some shared structure across relations. On the other hand, the causal direction learned from cross-sentence explanatory relations transfers strongly to unseen within-sentence elicitures, outperforming the occasion and plausibility directions in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2b}). Norm-matched random directions average near chance on these evaluations (Appendix~\ref{app:random-baselines}). These results indicate that models encode a causal signal that generalizes across syntactic constructions and is not reducible to the tested occasion or event-feasibility contrasts.

\subsection{Intervening on implicit causal representations}
\label{sec:intervention}
The projection results establish that the causal direction distinguishes critical and control items, but do not show whether changing activation along this direction affects model behavior. We therefore alter model activations along the direction and measure the change in surprisal for \emph{``I don't know why.''} If the direction encodes causal information, adding it to a control context should make this continuation more surprising, whereas subtracting it from a critical context should make the continuation less surprising.

\paragraph{Methods.}
For a causal CAA direction $v_l$ at the layer $l$ selected in Section~\ref{sec:representations}, we replace each non-padding activation $h_{l,t}$ with
\begin{equation}
    h'_{l,t}=h_{l,t}+\alpha v_l.
\end{equation}
We intervene at every token position while scoring the fixed continuation token by token. For every learned causal direction and each of ten norm-matched random directions, we test $|\alpha|\in\{.5,1,2,3,4,5\}$, subtracting the direction from critical contexts and adding it to their controls. At each magnitude, we measure the change in paired accuracy on the 150 within-sentence pairs relative to no intervention. A negative change indicates that the intervention reduces the model's behavioral discrimination between critical and control contexts. Confidence intervals bootstrap item pairs for the learned direction and jointly bootstrap directions and item pairs for the random baseline. Because we use raw, unnormalized directions, intervention magnitudes are interpreted within, rather than across, models; the norms of the selected directions vary substantially across models.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/intervention_alpha_sweep.pdf}
  \caption{Change in paired accuracy across fixed intervention magnitudes $|\alpha|=1$--$5$ for the causal direction and ten norm-matched random directions; points show mean changes and error bars show 95\% bootstrap CIs. Negative values indicate reduced discrimination between critical and control contexts. The causal-direction CI bootstraps the 150 item pairs; the random-direction CI jointly bootstraps directions and the shared item pairs. \textbf{In five models, increasing the causal intervention increasingly reduces paired accuracy and generally has a larger effect than the random baseline; Qwen3 4B shows no reliable effect, while Llama~3.1 70B changes in the opposite direction.}}
  \label{fig:intervention-alpha-sweep}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Results.}
The learned-direction effect becomes progressively more negative as intervention magnitude increases in five models (Figure~\ref{fig:intervention-alpha-sweep}). At $|\alpha|=5$, paired accuracy falls by 20.7--69.3 percentage points in these models. Qwen3 4B instead remains near its unmodified accuracy, while Llama~3.1 70B shows a small increase. The learned direction has a larger absolute effect than the mean random direction in six of seven models at the largest magnitude, although random directions also reduce accuracy in some models. The intervention therefore provides evidence that the causal direction can affect behavioral discrimination, but not that this effect is unique to the learned direction. Appendix~\ref{app:fixed-alpha-sweep} reports the numerical estimates.

\subsection{Implicit representations of causality in real-world scenarios}
\label{sec:naturalistic}
Having identified a causal direction that generalizes across linguistic constructions, we next examine whether the causal representation identified on controlled synthetic data also reflects potential real-world consequences. In particular, we test whether changing only a person’s demographic description alters how strongly the model represents that person as causally related to positive and negative social outcomes.

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We adapt 65 decision scenarios from \citet{christian-mazor-2026-selfblinding}, based on \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}. For each scenario, we construct positive and negative outcomes with four matched descriptions---a white man, a white woman, a minority man, or a minority woman---and a race/gender-agnostic baseline. The baseline replaces the demographic description with a manually reviewed incidental detail that does not suggest the outcome and is approximately matched in word length. For example, \emph{The white male salesperson was denied a work visa to move to the United States} is compared with \emph{The salesperson at the consulate was denied a work visa to move to the United States}; each has a positive counterpart in which the visa was granted. Minority identities are sampled from {Black, Hispanic, Asian}, yielding 650 sentences.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We use two complementary measures. First, we project each sentence's final-token activation onto the causal CAA direction identified in Section~\ref{sec:representations}; higher scores indicate stronger causal representations. To place models on a common descriptive scale, we divide each model's projections by its mean critical-minus-control projection gap on the 384 causal training pairs. Thus, one normalized unit equals that model's average training contrast. 

Second, we present the sentence and ask how strongly the person's demographic identity explains the outcome on the 1--7 scale from Section~\ref{sec:behavior}. For the agnostic condition, the question instead names its scenario-specific incidental detail. In Figure~\ref{fig:naturalistic-aggregate}, we subtract the matched white-male score from each remaining condition; colors distinguish positive and negative outcomes, and the corresponding dashed lines show the neutral-baseline-minus-white-male differences. We give each model equal weight by averaging the seven differences within a scenario and then averaging across the 65 scenarios; 95\% CIs bootstrap scenarios and treat the seven tested models as fixed. Appendix~\ref{app:naturalistic-model-results} additionally reports each demographic condition relative to the neutral baseline.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.9\textwidth]{figures/naturalistic_combined_scores_seven_model.pdf}
  \caption{Differences from the matched white-male condition for positive and negative outcomes, measured by training-gap-normalized causal projection (left) and expected Likert rating (right). Bars show equal-weight means across seven models with 95\% scenario-bootstrap CIs; green and coral dashed lines give the corresponding non-demographic-baseline-minus-white-male differences, and solid gray lines mark zero. \textbf{Positive-outcome CAA projections increase across demographic conditions, while minority women exceed white men in both negative-outcome measures.}}
  \label{fig:naturalistic-aggregate}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Results.}
Relative to the white-male condition, positive-outcome causal projections are higher for white women, minority men, and minority women, although the white-female difference is small (Figure~\ref{fig:naturalistic-aggregate}). Positive-outcome Likert ratings are reliably lower only for minority men. For negative outcomes, minority women receive higher causal projections and ratings; white women receive slightly lower causal projections, while the remaining contrasts include zero. The neutral baseline lies above white men for positive-outcome ratings and below white men for both negative-outcome measures, although this auxiliary comparison is sensitive to imperfect token-length matching. The per-model estimates vary substantially (Appendix~\ref{app:naturalistic-model-results}); for example, the negative-outcome projection differences from the neutral baseline reverse direction for Gemma~2 2B. Norm-matched random-direction contrasts are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:random-baselines}. Thus, the aggregate directions describe this fixed collection of models rather than a uniform effect in every model.

\section{Conclusion}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results highlight a distinction between \emph{making} an implicit inference and \emph{reporting} it. Implicit causality consistently shapes models' expectations about what comes next, even when direct judgments of the same relation are not consistently reliable. This motivates looking beyond direct responses when studying what LMs infer from context. The representation results further suggest that implicit causality is not tied to a single linguistic realization. A causal direction learned from cross-sentence relations remains informative for within-sentence elicitures and is more selective for causality than for plausibility or occasion compatibility. Intervening along this direction changes model expectations in most models, providing evidence that the representation is behaviorally consequential rather than only correlational. Demographic information can also change activation along the same causal direction: holding the social outcome fixed, changing only the demographic description shifts the strength of the causal signal in some models and conditions. Implicit causal representations may therefore provide a useful way to study how LMs encode unstated relations, both in everyday language and in scenarios with real-world consequences. % and how contextual information affects those representations.

\section*{Limitations}
Our experiments cover seven English-language base models and a controlled set of synthetic or adapted stimuli. The event-feasibility baseline combines physical, selectional, and animacy violations, so it does not exhaust possible representations of plausibility. CAA projections identify directions associated with the tested contrasts, but do not by themselves establish that those directions are causally necessary for model behavior. In the intervention experiment, equal values of $\alpha$ do not imply equal perturbation magnitudes across models because the learned directions are not normalized. The naturalistic evaluation uses coarse demographic categories and should not be interpreted as a comprehensive characterization of social bias. Its incidental baselines are close in word length but average about one model token longer than the demographic sentences, and the reported intervals are exploratory and uncorrected for multiple comparisons. Future work should test additional languages, model families, causal constructions, and naturalistic datasets.

\section{Behavioral evaluation details}
\label{app:behavior-details}

\paragraph{Yes/No prompt and score.}
The complete prompt is:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Context: [person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome]\\
Question: Does [modifier/relative clause] explain why [outcome]? Answer Yes or No:
\end{quote}
The modifier and outcome are paraphrased as standalone clauses where needed. Let $q$ denote the prompt. We score the capitalization variants $\mathcal{Y}=\{\texttt{yes},\texttt{Yes},\texttt{YES}\}$ and $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{no},\texttt{No},\texttt{NO}\}$, with one leading space prepended to every string and no terminal punctuation. If the tokenization of a candidate $a$ is $(a_1,\ldots,a_T)$, its complete-string probability is
\begin{equation}
P_\theta(a\mid q)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}
P_\theta(a_t\mid q,a_{<t}).
\label{eq:answer-sequence-probability}
\end{equation}
The Yes/No score renormalizes the summed probability mass of the Yes variants over all six candidates:
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{YN}}(q)=
\frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}}P_\theta(a\mid q)}
{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{N}}P_\theta(a\mid q)}.
\label{eq:yes-probability}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{7-point Likert prompt and score.}
For the same item, the complete prompt is:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Context: [person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome].\\
Question: How strongly does [modifier/relative clause] explain why [outcome]? Answer with one integer from 1 to 7, where 1 means definitely not, 4 means uncertain, and 7 means definitely yes:
\end{quote}
Let $a_k$ be the complete answer string consisting of one leading space followed by the integer $k$. Using the complete-string probability in Equation~\ref{eq:answer-sequence-probability}, we normalize over the seven candidates and take their expected value:
\begin{align}
\pi_k(q)
  &=\frac{P_\theta(a_k\mid q)}
  {\sum_{j=1}^{7}P_\theta(a_j\mid q)},\\
s_{\mathrm{L}}(q)&=\sum_{k=1}^{7}k\,\pi_k(q).
\label{eq:Likert-score}
\end{align}

\paragraph{IDK Why surprisal score.}
For the critical member of the example pair, the prefix $x$ is the complete sentence [person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome], including its final period. We then append a space followed by \emph{I don't know why.} The resulting sequence has the form [person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome].\emph{ I don't know why.} For continuation tokens $c=(c_1,\ldots,c_M)$, summed continuation surprisal is
\begin{equation}
S(c\mid x)=-\sum_{t=1}^{M}\log P_\theta(c_t\mid x,c_{<t}).
\label{eq:surprisal}
\end{equation}
This sum includes every token in the appended string, including its final period. A larger value means that the continuation is less expected.

\paragraph{Because-And surprisal score.}
For each item, we reconstruct two sentence-only versions from the outcome event $E$ and modifier content $F$: \emph{$E$ because $F$} and \emph{$E$, and $F$}, each ending in a period. For example, the critical item becomes \emph{The executive was fired because the executive falsified quarterly revenue figures.} versus \emph{The executive was fired, and the executive falsified quarterly revenue figures.} We score the identical tokens of $F$, including the final period, after each connective. For target tokens $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_M)$, the score is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\ell_{\mathrm{because},t}
  &=\log P_\theta(f_t\mid E\ \text{because},f_{<t}),\\
\ell_{\mathrm{and},t}
  &=\log P_\theta(f_t\mid E\text{, and},f_{<t}),\\
B(E,F)&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=1}^{M}
  \left(\ell_{\mathrm{because},t}-\ell_{\mathrm{and},t}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:because-and}
\end{equation}
A larger value means that the model favors a causal relation between the events over simple conjunction.

\paragraph{Candidate scoring and paired accuracy.}
All candidate probabilities are computed from the model logits at temperature 1. We score the candidates directly rather than sampling generated responses, so no decoding parameters are used. For each measure $m$ ($s_{\mathrm{YN}}$, $s_{\mathrm{L}}$, $S$, or $B$) and $N$ item pairs, we compute
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{pair}}(m)
  &=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[
    m(x_i^{\mathrm{crit}})>m(x_i^{\mathrm{ctrl}})
  \right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:paired-accuracy}
\end{equation}
Ties receive zero credit.
\section{CAA extraction and evaluation details}
\label{app:caa-details}

\subsection{Causal CAA direction extraction}
\label{app:caa-extraction}

For each model and candidate layer $\ell$, we extract the activation at the
final non-padding token of the raw stimulus text. Let
$\mathbf{h}^{+}_{i,\ell}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{-}_{i,\ell}$ denote the activations
for the critical and control members of training pair $i$. The CAA direction is
the mean paired activation difference
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\ell}
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left(\mathbf{h}^{+}_{i,\ell}-\mathbf{h}^{-}_{i,\ell}\right).
\label{eq:caa-direction}
\end{equation}
Directions are computed independently at layers 4, 8,
12, and so on with a stride of 4, and the model's final layer is included when it does not fall on
this stride.

\subsection{CAA projection and evaluation}
\label{app:caa-evaluation}

We score an unseen stimulus activation $\mathbf{h}_{j,\ell}$ by its projection
onto the unit-normalized direction:
\begin{equation}
s_{j,\ell}
=\mathbf{h}_{j,\ell}^{\top}
\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\ell}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\rVert_2}.
\label{eq:caa-projection}
\end{equation}
For cross-model naturalistic summaries, we use
$\widetilde{s}_{j,\ell}=s_{j,\ell}/g_m$, where
$g_m=\operatorname{mean}_{i}(s^+_{i,\ell}-s^-_{i,\ell})$ over the 384 causal
training pairs for model $m$. Because $\mathbf{v}_{\ell}$ is their mean
activation difference, $g_m=\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\rVert_2$. Random-direction
naturalistic controls use the same learned-direction $g_m$ rather than a
random-direction-specific denominator.
For each matched evaluation pair, we calculate
$d_i=s^{+}_{i,\ell}-s^{-}_{i,\ell}$. Paired accuracy is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{pair}}
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[\mathbf{1}(d_i>0)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}(d_i=0)\right].
\label{eq:caa-paired-accuracy}
\end{equation}
We select the layer used for test-time CAA projection by taking the layer with the highest paired accuracy on the developmental set among layers 4, 8, 12, etc.
