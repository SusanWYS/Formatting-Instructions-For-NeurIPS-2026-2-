\begin{abstract}
Humans often infer causal relationships that are suggested but never explicitly stated. For example, “Melissa detests the children who are arrogant and rude” suggests that Melissa detests the children \emph{because} they are arrogant and rude. We ask whether language models make similar implicit causal inferences and whether these inferences are represented internally. Across six language models, we find that implicit causal information reliably changes what models expect to come next, even when models do not always report the same inference when asked directly. We also find an internal representation of causality that can be distinguished from plausibility and occasion compatibility. This representation transfers from explicit causal relations across sentences to implicit causal relations within a sentence. Moreover, this representation may support causal readings in scenarios with real-world consequences: causal signal is stronger for minority identities as subjects to negative social scenarios, especially minority women, than for white men. Together, these results suggest that implicit causal inferences are reflected both in model behavior and in internal representations that generalize across linguistic contexts and reveal systematic differences in socially implicated settings.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Language often leaves relationships between events unstated. For example, ``The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired'' suggests that the falsification \emph{caused} the firing, without explicitly stating so. These optional sentence-internal inferences are known as \emph{elicitures} \citep{cohen-kehler-2021-conversational}. While a susbtantial body of work has investigated whether language models (LMs) capture pragmatic phenomena such as conversational implicatures \citep{ruis2023goldilocks, jeretic-etal-2020-natural, schuster-etal-2020-harnessing, li-etal-2021-predicting}, elicitures have received less attention \citep[but see][]{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}, and are potentially derived through different mechanisms. Some theories argue that, in contrast to implicature, elicitures arise due to coherence and world knowledge rather than violation of conversational norms \citep{cohen-kehler-2021-conversational,rohde-etal-2011-anticipating}. Therefore, understanding how LMs represent implicit causal relationships can shed light on their internal world models---including in scenarios where causal inferences have real-world impacts.

Here, we explore three main research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} \textit{Do LM behaviors reflect inferences about implicit causal relationships within sentences?} \textbf{RQ2:} \textit{Do LMs form internal representations of implicit causal relationships?} And if so, \textbf{RQ3:} \textit{Do representations of implicit causality generalize to settings with potential real-world implications?}

In a related study, \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic} show that LMs infer implicit causality via eliciture and use it during subsequent sentence processing (akin to our RQ1). This project extends Pan \& Kehler's behavioral study by %focusing on causal elicitures (instead of temporal relationships or other discourse relationships) and 
testing different syntactic constructions. Furthermore, we also study whether implicit causality forms a distinct, generalizable internal representation (RQ2), and whether this representation underlies inferences about demographic information and social biases (RQ3).

Across six language models tested, we find that implicit causality consistently shapes model behavior and is reflected in internal representations that generalize across linguistic constructions. These representations also vary systematically with demographic descriptions. More broadly, this study reveals how LMs represent causal relationships that are inferred rather than stated, 
% Our results can shed light on how models construct internal world models, how their nonliteral inferences align with human cognition, and whether these representations encode systematic biases with 
with potential implications for the development of safer and more aligned models.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Measuring implicit causal inference through model behaviors}
\label{sec:behavior}
We first ask whether implicit causal inferences influence model behavior. To test this, we compare two kinds of explicit metalinguistic judgments with an implicit measure based on the surprisal of subsequent text \citep{hu-levy-2023-prompting}. Our setup replicates and extends \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}. More details about behavioral evaluation are provided in \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.

\paragraph{Methods.} 

We manually authored 150 novel minimal pairs (critical/control) with sentences of the following structure: \textbf{[person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome]}. The modifier plausibly causes the outcome in a critical sentence (e.g., \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired}) but is incidental in its control (e.g., \emph{The executive who wore a blue silk tie was fired}). 

We consider three evaluation methods. Under the \textbf{Yes/No (metalinguistic)} method, we present the sentence and append the question, ``Does [modifier] explain why [outcome]?'' We normalize complete-answer probabilities to obtain $P(\text{Yes})$. % The prompt is in \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.
Under the \textbf{Likert (metalinguistic)} method, we present the sentence and ask how strongly the modifier explains the outcome from 1 (definitely not) through 4 (uncertain) to 7 (definitely yes). The rating is the probability-weighted mean of the seven complete answers. % ; see \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.
Finally, under the \textbf{surprisal (implicit)} method, we score the surprisal of \emph{", and I don't know why."} conditioned on the sentence, following \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}. If the modifier supplies an explanation, this continuation should be less expected. % See \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.
For all tasks, we report paired accuracy, which is the proportion of critical items scoring strictly above their controls. See Appendix~\ref{app:behavior-details} for details.

In all experiments, we test Gemma~2 2B/9B \citep{gemma-team-2024-gemma2}, Qwen3 4B/8B \citep{qwen-team-2025-qwen3}, Llama~3.2 3B \citep{meta-2024-llama32}, and Llama~3 8B \citep{dubey-etal-2024-llama3} base checkpoints.

Thus, whether a model appears to recognize an implicit causal relation depends strongly on how the inference is elicited: causal information can robustly affect next-token expectations even when the model does not reliably report the same inference metalinguistically.

\subsection{Identifying internal representations of implicit causality inference}
\label{sec:representations}

The behavioral results show that implicit causal relations affect model predictions, but they do not reveal how the inference is represented in model internals. Therefore, we ask two subsequent questions: 1) Is implicit causality selectively represented in LM activation states? and 2) Can a representation learned from cross-sentence causal relationships generalize to the weaker, non-mandated causal readings of within-sentence inferences?

\paragraph{Datasets.} We use three datasets of matched critical/control pairs (see \hyperref[app:representation-examples]{Appendix~\ref*{app:representation-examples}} for examples).
First, the main \textbf{Causal} dataset (480 pairs) tests implicit causality \emph{across} sentences. We use \emph{cross-sentence} causality because many theories propose that clauses in discourse (here, sentences) must be structurally related \citep[e.g.,][]{hobbs_literature_1990,kehler_coherence_2002}, in contrast to the non-mandated causal relationships between elements \emph{within} a sentence. This approach allows us to test whether causality representations learned from discourse relationships generalizes to within-sentence causality (i.e., eliciture). In each pair of sentences, an \emph{outcome sentence} is followed by either an \emph{explanation or incidental detail sentence}. 
% We constructed 480 pairs: 384 training, 48 development, and 48 test. %held-out in-distribution test. 
For out-of-distribution evaluation, we use the 150 within-sentence pairs from \Cref{sec:behavior}. % See \hyperref[app:representation-examples]{Appendix~\ref*{app:representation-examples}} for examples.
Second, the \textbf{Occasion (baseline)} dataset (1200 pairs) tests whether an event is compatible with a person's typical activity. We adapt and expand the occasion items from \citet{hu-rubiofernandez-2026-referential}.

Finally, the \textbf{Plausibility (baseline)} dataset (70 pairs) contrasts probable with physically impossible events, which we adapt from \citet{hu-etal-2025-shades} and \citet{lepori-etal-2026-fantasy}.

We use \emph{Occasion} and \emph{Plausibility} as baselines because both require world knowledge but do not encode causal relations. Specifically, \emph{Occasion} evokes another type of non-causal eliciture as described in \citet{hu-rubiofernandez-2026-referential}. They therefore help test whether the causal direction captures causality specifically, rather than general semantic compatibility or event likelihood.
\paragraph{CAA directions.} We use contrastive activation addition (CAA) \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering} to identify activation-space directions associated with each dataset. At each layer, for every matched training pair, we subtract the final-token activation of the control example from that of the critical example and average these differences to obtain a single direction. We then project unseen critical and control examples onto this direction: if the direction captures the target relation, the critical example should receive a higher projection than its matched control. For each training-direction/evaluation-dataset combination, we select the layer with the highest paired accuracy on the development set and report accuracy on the disjoint test set.

\paragraph{Results.}
The CAA directions are relatively relation-selective: each direction strongly separates its own held-out relation, whereas cross-relation transfer is substantially weaker and more variable (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-transfer}). This selectivity is not absolute, indicating some shared structure across relations. On the other hand, the causal direction learned from explicit cross-sentence explanations transfers nearly perfectly to unseen within-sentence elicitures, outperforming the occasion and plausibility directions in every model (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2b}). These results indicate that models encode a causal signal that generalizes across syntactic constructions and cannot be explained solely by occasion or event plausibility.

\subsection{Implicit representations of causality in real-world scenarios}
\label{sec:naturalistic}
Having identified a causal direction that generalizes across linguistic constructions, we next examine whether the causal representation identified on controlled synthetic data also reflects systematic differences in scenarios with potential real-world consequences. In particular, we test whether changing only a person’s demographic description alters how strongly the model represents that person as causally related to a negative social outcome.

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We adapt 65 decision scenarios from \citet{christian-mazor-2026-selfblinding}, based on \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}. For each scenario, we construct three matched versions describing a white man, a minority man, or a minority woman, while keeping the occupation and outcome identical. For example, \emph{The white male salesperson was denied a work visa} is compared with \emph{The Black male salesperson was denied a work visa} and \emph{The Black female salesperson was denied a work visa}. Minority identities are sampled from {Black, Hispanic, Asian}. This yields 195 sentences in total, listed in \hyperref[app:naturalistic-stimuli]{Appendix~\ref*{app:naturalistic-stimuli}}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We use the causal CAA direction identified in \Cref{sec:representations} as a measure of how strongly a model activation expresses causal information. For each sentence, we project its final-token activation onto this direction; a higher projection indicates that the activation more strongly resembles the representation associated with causal. Within each scenario, we subtract the minority-male or minority-female projections from the matched white-male projection. We then average these paired differences across the 65 scenarios and report 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Results.}
Changing only the demographic description produces a consistent shift along the causal direction. In all six models, mean projections follow the same ordering: minority female $>$ minority male $>$ white male (\Cref{tab:rq3}). Minority-female conditions have significantly higher causal projections than the white-male conditions in every model, while minority-male differences are positive in all models. Distributions of the projection scores are provided in \Cref{fig:rq3-distributions}. We emphasize that the causal direction was learned via descriptions of commonplace events, without any explicit references to race or gender. These results suggest that demographic information can systematically alter how models internally represent the causes of negative outcomes.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results highlight a distinction between \emph{making} an implicit inference and \emph{reporting} it. Implicit causality consistently shapes models' expectations about what comes next, even when direct judgments of the same relation are unreliable. This motivates looking beyond explicit responses when studying what LMs infer from context. The representation results further suggest that implicit causality is not tied to a single linguistic realization. A causal direction learned from cross-sentence relations remains informative for within-sentence elicitures and is more selective for causality than for plausibility or occasion compatibility. Demographic information also systematically changes activation along the same causal direction: holding the adverse outcome fixed, changing only the demographic description shifts the strength of the causal signal. Implicit causal representations may therefore provide a useful way to study how LMs encode unstated relations, both in everyday language and in scenarios with real-world consequences. % and how contextual information affects those representations.

\section{Behavioral Evaluation Details}
\label{app:behavior-details}

\paragraph{Yes/No prompt and score.}
For the critical sentence, the complete prompt is:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Context: The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired.\\
Question: Does the fact that the executive falsified quarterly revenue figures explain why the executive was fired? Answer Yes or No:
\end{quote}
The control prompt replaces both occurrences of \emph{falsified quarterly revenue figures} with \emph{wore a blue silk tie}. Let $q$ denote a prompt and $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_T)$ a candidate answer token sequence. We compute its complete sequence probability as
\begin{equation}
P_\theta(a\mid q)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_\theta(a_t\mid q,a_{<t})\right).
\label{eq:answer-probability}
\end{equation}
We score the leading-space variants \texttt{ yes}, \texttt{ Yes}, and \texttt{ YES}, and the corresponding three variants of \texttt{no}. If $\mathcal{Y}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ are these two sets, the Yes/No score is
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{yes}}(q)=
\frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}}P_\theta(a\mid q)}
{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{N}}P_\theta(a\mid q)}.
\label{eq:yes-probability}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{7-point Likert prompt and score.}
For the same critical sentence, the complete prompt is:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Context: The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired.\\
Question: How strongly does the fact that the executive falsified quarterly revenue figures explain why the executive was fired? Answer with one integer from 1 to 7, where 1 means definitely not, 4 means uncertain, and 7 means definitely yes:
\end{quote}
We compute the complete sequence probability in \autoref{eq:answer-probability} for each leading-space answer \texttt{ 1}, \ldots, \texttt{ 7}. We normalize over these seven candidates and take their expected value:
\begin{align}
\widetilde P_\theta(k\mid q)
  &=\frac{P_\theta(\texttt{ }k\mid q)}{\sum_{j=1}^{7}P_\theta(\texttt{ }j\mid q)},
  &r(q)&=\sum_{k=1}^{7}k\,\widetilde P_\theta(k\mid q).
\label{eq:likert-score}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Surprisal score.}
For the critical member of the example pair, we score the continuation after the prefix \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired}; the full sequence is \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired, and I don't know why.} For continuation tokens $c=(c_1,\ldots,c_M)$ and stimulus prefix $x$, summed continuation surprisal is
\begin{equation}
S(c\mid x)=-\sum_{t=1}^{M}\log P_\theta(c_t\mid x,c_{<t}).
\label{eq:surprisal}
\end{equation}
This sum includes every token in \emph{", and I don't know why."}, including punctuation. A larger value means that the continuation is less expected.

\paragraph{Paired accuracy and sampling parameters.}
For each measure $m$, we compute
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{pair}}(m)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[m(x_i^{\mathrm{critical}})>m(x_i^{\mathrm{control}})\right].
\label{eq:paired-accuracy}
\end{equation}
Here $m$ is $p_{\mathrm{yes}}$, $r$, or $S$; ties receive zero credit. Yes/No and Likert responses are not sampled. We score complete candidate sequences directly from the base model's raw logits with temperature 1; top-$p$ and top-$k$ sampling are therefore not applied.

\section{CAA Extraction and Evaluation Details}
\label{app:caa-details}

\subsection{Causal CAA Direction Extraction}
\label{app:caa-extraction}

For each model and candidate layer $\ell$, we extract the activation at the
final non-padding token of the raw stimulus text. Let
$\mathbf{h}^{+}_{i,\ell}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{-}_{i,\ell}$ denote the activations
for the critical and control members of training pair $i$. The CAA direction is
the mean paired activation difference
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\ell}
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left(\mathbf{h}^{+}_{i,\ell}-\mathbf{h}^{-}_{i,\ell}\right).
\label{eq:caa-direction}
\end{equation}
For the causal direction, the positive member contains an explanatory
cross-sentence relation and the negative member replaces the explanation with
an incidental detail. Directions are computed independently at layers 4, 8,
12, and so on with a stride of 4, and the model's final layer is included when it does not fall on
this stride. No test examples contribute to direction construction.

\subsection{CAA Evaluation and Layer Selection}
\label{app:caa-evaluation}

We score an unseen stimulus activation $\mathbf{h}_{j,\ell}$ by its projection
onto the unit-normalized direction:
\begin{equation}
s_{j,\ell}
=\mathbf{h}_{j,\ell}^{\top}
\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\ell}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\rVert_2}.
\label{eq:caa-projection}
\end{equation}
For each matched evaluation pair, we calculate
$d_i=s^{+}_{i,\ell}-s^{-}_{i,\ell}$. Paired accuracy is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{pair}}
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[\mathbf{1}(d_i>0)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}(d_i=0)\right].
\label{eq:caa-paired-accuracy}
\end{equation}

The causal, occasion, and plausibility datasets are partitioned by matched pair
before activation extraction. Their train/development/test sizes are
384/48/48, 800/200/200, and 42/14/14 pairs, respectively. For each
training-direction/evaluation-dataset combination, we select the layer with the
highest development paired accuracy; exact ties are resolved in favor of the
earliest layer. We then evaluate that fixed direction and layer once on the
corresponding disjoint test set. For the 150-pair within-sentence evaluation in
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2b}, every source direction uses the layer selected on the
causal development set, so the within-sentence items are never used for layer
selection. The naturalistic evaluation likewise applies each model's fixed
causal direction and selected layer, using the unit-normalized projections in
Equation~\ref{eq:caa-projection} for the paired demographic comparisons.
