\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Language often leaves relationships between events unstated. For example, \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired} suggests that the falsification caused the firing without explicitly stating it. Such defeasible inferences are \emph{elicitures} \citep{cohen-kehler-2021-conversational}. Testing whether LMs draw these inferences would inform us what world knowledge they encode. 

\citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic} show that LMs infer elicitures and use them during subsequent sentence processing. Yet a model may represent a relationship without reporting it under a particular prompt. We extend Pan \& Kehler's  behavioral study across model families and test whether implicit causality forms a distinct, reusable internal representation.

We explore three main questions: 
\begin{itemize}[label=, noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
\item
\textbf{RQ1} \textit{Do model behaviors reflect inferences about implicit causal relationships within sentences? }
\item \textbf{RQ2} \textit{Do LMs form internal representations of implicit causal relationships?}
\item \textbf{RQ3} \textit{If \textbf{RQ2} is true, do representations of implicit causality generalize to naturalistic settings with potential real-world implications?}
\end{itemize}

The benefits of understanding elicitures in LMs are multifold: 1. more comprehensive views into how LMs build an internal world model 2. knowledge of the cognitive alignment between LMs and humans in nonliteral and non-mandated inference 3. understanding of the implicit bias in LMs, which has implications in safety and alignment.

\subsection{Measuring Implicit Causal Inference through Model Behaviors}
\label{sec:behavior}

\textbf{Models.} In all experiments, we test Gemma~2 2B/9B \citep{gemma-team-2024-gemma2}, Qwen3 4B/8B \citep{qwen-team-2025-qwen3}, Llama~3.2 3B \citep{meta-2024-llama32}, and Llama~3 8B \citep{dubey-etal-2024-llama3} base checkpoints.

\textbf{Dataset.} We use 150 manually authored minimal pairs following [person] [modifier/relative clause] [outcome]. The modifier plausibly causes the outcome in a critical sentence but is incidental in its control. For example, \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired} is paired with \emph{The executive who wore a blue silk tie was fired}. See \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}} for examples.

\textbf{Yes/No.} After presenting the sentence as context, we ask, ``Does [modifier] explain why [outcome]?'' We normalize complete-answer probabilities, including capitalization variants, to obtain $P(\text{Yes})$. The prompt is in \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.

\textbf{7-point Likert.} After the same context, we ask how strongly the modifier explains the outcome from 1 (definitely not) through 4 (uncertain) to 7 (definitely yes). The rating is the probability-weighted mean of the seven complete answers; see \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.

\textbf{Surprisal.} Following \citet{pan-kehler-2025-pragmatic}, we append \emph{", and I don't know why."} and score its surprisal. If the modifier supplies an explanation, this continuation should be less expected. See \hyperref[app:behavior-details]{Appendix~\ref*{app:behavior-details}}.

For each measure, paired accuracy is the proportion of critical items scoring strictly above their controls; chance is $.5$, and ties are incorrect.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.72\linewidth]{figures/rq1_behavior.pdf}
  \caption{Paired accuracy of model judgments on implicit causal inference items, comparing Yes/No prompting, 7-point likert rating, and the surprisal of the continuation \textit{", and I don't know why."} Higher accuracy indicates better performance in implicit causality inferences.}
  \label{fig:rq1}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Results.} In \autoref{fig:rq1}, surprisal identifies implicit causality most consistently. Direct Yes/No and Likert performance varies more across models and generally improves with model size.


\subsection{Identifying Internal Representations of Implicit Causality Inference}
\label{sec:representations}

We ask whether model activations distinguish causality from occasion and plausibility, and whether a direction learned from cross-sentence causality transfers to the weaker, non-mandated causal readings of within-sentence modifiers.

\textbf{Datasets.} We use three datasets of matched critical/control pairs:

1) \emph{Causal} tests implicit causality across sentences. In 480 pairs, an outcome is followed by either an explanation (critical) or incidental detail (control): 384 training, 48 development, and 48 held-out in-distribution test pairs. For out-of-distribution evaluation, we use the 150 within-sentence pairs from \autoref{sec:behavior}. See \hyperref[app:representation-examples]{Appendix~\ref*{app:representation-examples}} for examples.

2) \emph{Occasion} tests whether an event is compatible with a person's typical activity. We adapt and expand the occasion items from [cite Jenn's paper]. Of 1,200 pairs, 800 are for training, 200 for development, and 200 for held-out in-distribution testing. See \hyperref[app:representation-examples]{Appendix~\ref*{app:representation-examples}}.

3) \emph{Plausibility} contrasts probable with physically impossible events. We adapt 70 pairs from \citet{hu-etal-2025-shades}, following \citet{lepori-etal-2026-fantasy}: 42 training, 14 development, and 14 held-out in-distribution test pairs. See \hyperref[app:representation-examples]{Appendix~\ref*{app:representation-examples}}.

\textbf{CAA directions.} CAA represents a relation as a direction in activation space \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering}. At each layer, we average the difference between matched critical and control final-token activations. For each training-direction/evaluation-dataset combination, we select the layer on the development set and report paired accuracy only on the disjoint test set.

\paragraph{Results.}
On the held-out tests in \autoref{fig:rq2-transfer}, causal directions reach $1.00$ paired accuracy in every model; occasion directions reach $.90$--$.98$ and plausibility directions $.79$--$.93$ on their own relations. Cross-relation accuracy is lower and more variable ($.27$--$.86$). On the within-sentence items in \autoref{fig:rq2b}, causal directions reach $.91$--$.99$ and outperform the other two directions in every model. Thus, causal information generalizes across syntax without reducing to occasion or plausibility.
\subsection{Generalization to Naturalistic Scenarios}
\label{sec:naturalistic}
Finally, we ask whether causal representations learned from synthetic data generalize to scenarios with real-world implications.

\textbf{Dataset.} 
This dataset tests whether demographic descriptions affect implicit causal interpretations of negative outcomes. We adapt 65 decision scenarios from \citet{christian-mazor-2026-selfblinding}, based on \citet{tamkin-etal-2023-discrimination}. Each has matched white-male, minority-male, and minority-female conditions; only the demographic description changes. For example, \emph{The white male salesperson was denied a work visa} is paired with \emph{The [minority] \{male, female\} salesperson was denied a work visa}, where [minority] is sampled from \{Black, Hispanic, Asian\}. The 195 sentences are listed in \hyperref[app:naturalistic-stimuli]{Appendix~\ref*{app:naturalistic-stimuli}}.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We project each final-token activation onto the causal CAA direction from \autoref{sec:representations}. Within each scenario, we subtract the white-male projection from each minority condition, average across scenarios, and report 95\% bootstrap CIs.

In \autoref{tab:rq3}, minority-female projections exceed white-male projections in all six models. Minority-male differences are positive, with CIs excluding zero in four models. Thus, causal representations remain sensitive to demographic descriptions in naturalistic settings.

\section{Behavioral Evaluation Details}
\label{app:behavior-details}

\paragraph{Qualitative example.}
One item pairs the critical sentence \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired} with the control sentence \emph{The executive who wore a blue silk tie was fired}. The outcome is identical across the pair. Only the modifier changes: falsifying revenue figures plausibly explains the firing, whereas wearing a blue tie is incidental.

\paragraph{Yes/No prompt and score.}
For the critical sentence, the complete prompt is:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Context: The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired.\\
Question: Does the fact that the executive falsified quarterly revenue figures explain why the executive was fired? Answer Yes or No:
\end{quote}
The control prompt replaces both occurrences of \emph{falsified quarterly revenue figures} with \emph{wore a blue silk tie}. Let $q$ denote a prompt and $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_T)$ a candidate answer token sequence. We compute its complete sequence probability as
\begin{equation}
P_\theta(a\mid q)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_\theta(a_t\mid q,a_{<t})\right).
\label{eq:answer-probability}
\end{equation}
We score the leading-space variants \texttt{ yes}, \texttt{ Yes}, and \texttt{ YES}, and the corresponding three variants of \texttt{no}. If $\mathcal{Y}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ are these two sets, the Yes/No score is
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{yes}}(q)=
\frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}}P_\theta(a\mid q)}
{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{N}}P_\theta(a\mid q)}.
\label{eq:yes-probability}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{7-point Likert prompt and score.}
For the same critical sentence, the complete prompt is:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Context: The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired.\\
Question: How strongly does the fact that the executive falsified quarterly revenue figures explain why the executive was fired? Answer with one integer from 1 to 7, where 1 means definitely not, 4 means uncertain, and 7 means definitely yes:
\end{quote}
We compute the complete sequence probability in \autoref{eq:answer-probability} for each leading-space answer \texttt{ 1}, \ldots, \texttt{ 7}. We normalize over these seven candidates and take their expected value:
\begin{align}
\widetilde P_\theta(k\mid q)
  &=\frac{P_\theta(\texttt{ }k\mid q)}{\sum_{j=1}^{7}P_\theta(\texttt{ }j\mid q)},
  &r(q)&=\sum_{k=1}^{7}k\,\widetilde P_\theta(k\mid q).
\label{eq:likert-score}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Surprisal score.}
For the critical member of the example pair, we score the continuation after the prefix \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired}; the full sequence is \emph{The executive who falsified quarterly revenue figures was fired, and I don't know why.} For continuation tokens $c=(c_1,\ldots,c_M)$ and stimulus prefix $x$, summed continuation surprisal is
\begin{equation}
S(c\mid x)=-\sum_{t=1}^{M}\log P_\theta(c_t\mid x,c_{<t}).
\label{eq:surprisal}
\end{equation}
This sum includes every token in \emph{", and I don't know why."}, including punctuation. A larger value means that the continuation is less expected.

\paragraph{Paired accuracy and sampling parameters.}
For each measure $m$, we compute
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{pair}}(m)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[m(x_i^{\mathrm{critical}})>m(x_i^{\mathrm{control}})\right].
\label{eq:paired-accuracy}
\end{equation}
Here $m$ is $p_{\mathrm{yes}}$, $r$, or $S$; ties receive zero credit. Yes/No and Likert responses are not sampled. We score complete candidate sequences directly from the base model's raw logits with temperature 1; top-$p$ and top-$k$ sampling are therefore not applied.

\section{Examples for the Representation Datasets}
\label{app:representation-examples}

\paragraph{Causal.}
In a critical pair member, the second sentence provides a potential explanation: \emph{Anika avoided the Amber Cafe. The Amber Cafe repeatedly mixed up Anika's allergy order.} In the matched control, it provides an incidental detail: \emph{Anika avoided the Amber Cafe. The Amber Cafe had a copper sign.}

\paragraph{Occasion.}
In a critical pair member, the event is compatible with the person's typical activity: \emph{A lifeguard got a painful sunburn last Sunday.} The matched control changes only the person to make that connection less expected: \emph{A translator got a painful sunburn last Sunday.}

\paragraph{Plausibility.}
In a critical pair member, the event is probable: \emph{Someone baked a cake inside an oven.} The matched control makes the event physically implausible: \emph{Someone baked a cake inside a freezer.}
